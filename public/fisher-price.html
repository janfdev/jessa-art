07 · The question
0 × 0

Where my career started.

Four years of designing and building playful digital experiences for every brand under the Fisher-Price umbrella. This was before mobile standards, before social was a given, before UX was even a job title.

My title said interactive graphic designer. What I actually did was the whole pipeline: storyboard the experience, design the visuals, animate the moments, then build the front end myself in HTML5 while Flash wound down. A website could genuinely play, and I loved that.

12 · Marketing to meaning
0 × 0

Translating marketing into meaningful experiences.

Most projects started as a marketing request. Promote a new toy, grow a brand, drive retail traffic. The real work started when I asked what it meant for the parent, and what it meant for the child.

The core value.What does the toy actually do? Learning, motor skills, imagination, social play.
The audience.Which age group, and should parent and child experience it together or apart?
The behavior.Play, explore, share, purchase. Every screen designed back from the action we wanted.

14 · Three designers
0 × 0

One brand, three designers, no Figma.

Three designers with different skills and taste, all making work that had to look like one hand made it, before collaborative tools existed. Everyone worked in Photoshop. The discipline was the chain: build, peer review, director review, marketing. That chain, not tooling, is how dozens of campaigns stayed unmistakably Fisher-Price.

Sometimes the job was getting ahead of the brand’s comfort zone. When I brought the hamburger menu to the site, marketing worried moms wouldn’t recognize it, so they put a “MENU” label under it. Once the pattern became a web standard, the label quietly went away.

36 · Looking back
0 × 0

The craft was strong. The measurement was missing.

Performance data never made it back to designers. UX barely existed as a discipline, and we weren’t part of that conversation. With the engagement data we now treat as standard, I would have closed the loop: tested gameplay flows, iterated on what kids actually played longest, and let real behavior drive the next version instead of brand instinct alone. That gap is exactly what drew me toward UX.
